2.2 Theoretical Background

The conceptualization of the MADN framework relies on several key technological paradigms:

•	Edge Computing and the Internet of Things (IoT): Edge computing and IoT offer a pathway to bring data processing closer to the source of data generation \[8, 31]. By shifting the computational burden from fragile external cloud networks to a resilient, community-driven 1 edge ecosystem, systems can dynamically process complex data locally \[1, 2, 18]. 

•	Distributed and Latent Computing: The proliferation of personal computing devices, such as smartphones, tablets, and laptops, has transformed the digital landscape \[28]. For most of the day, the processors inside these personal devices sit idle, representing a latent, decentralized computing capacity that is currently wasted \[3, 29]. By creating a collaborative network where idle consumer devices can temporarily lend their processing power, communities can essentially build their own localized micro-clouds \[9, 32].

•	Embedded Machine Learning: The deployment of lightweight machine learning models at the edge, such as TensorFlow Lite for agricultural yield prediction, forms the basis for extracting actionable intelligence without cloud dependency \[19, 20, 21].



Although these paradigms are closely related, the literature distinguishes them in analytically important ways. Edge computing emphasizes placing computation near data sources to reduce latency and improve responsiveness; distributed computing concerns the coordination of multiple computational nodes; latent computing focuses on mobilizing otherwise idle devices; and hybrid edge–cloud models address how these resources can be orchestrated across different infrastructure layers \[8, 9, 16, 31, 32]. Recognizing these distinctions is important for MADN because the framework does not depend on a single paradigm in isolation. Rather, it combines locality of processing, cooperation among heterogeneous devices, opportunistic use of idle resources, and selective access to cloud services into a unified architectural logic \[16, 31, 33].



From this perspective, MADN should be understood not as an edge-only architecture, but as a hybrid edge–cloud system operating across a broader computing continuum. Its primary logic remains local and edge-oriented so that latency-sensitive, connectivity-critical, and context-dependent functions can continue operating close to users and data sources. However, whenever network conditions, bandwidth, and service availability permit, the framework can also leverage cloud infrastructure for computationally intensive analytics, long-term data retention, cross-node synchronization, model training, and elastic scaling of services beyond the limitations of local hardware. This hybrid orientation is important because cloud platforms provide rapid elasticity, resource pooling, and on-demand access to high-capacity compute and storage resources, while edge layers provide responsiveness, locality, and operational resilience under constrained conditions \[31, 33, 36, 37]. In practical terms, this means that tasks such as immediate sensing, local inference, temporary data buffering, and continuity of transactions are better retained near the edge, whereas aggregated analytics, historical archiving, multi-node synchronization, and computationally intensive model refinement may be shifted to cloud infrastructure when connectivity conditions are favourable \[6, 12, 19, 31, 36, 37]. Accordingly, the architectural value of MADN lies not in rejecting cloud computing, but in selectively integrating cloud capabilities when they enhance flexibility, coordination, and overall system capability without undermining offline resilience \[31, 33, 37]. 



In innovation studies, a technological paradigm refers to a shared model of problem framing and 2 solution searches that channels technical development along particular trajectories, while ecosystem scholarship emphasizes the interdependent constellation of actors, artifacts, and complementarities through which value is created and captured \[38, 39]. Read together, these perspectives suggest that a technological ecosystem can be understood as the organizational and material instantiation of a paradigm, because the rules, interfaces, and complementarities of an ecosystem shape what kinds of innovation become visible, feasible, and scalable \[38, 39, 45]. Mature platform ecosystems illustrate this dynamic clearly: ecosystems such as Apple’s App Store and Android provide not only distribution channels but also development rules, governance mechanisms, and technical dependencies that condition how developers search for solutions and bring them to market \[39, 40, 41]. 



In the case of edge computing, the search space is similarly conditioned by the infrastructural logic of locality, latency sensitivity, and constrained device resources \[31, 33, 37]. Developers working in edge-oriented environments therefore tend to prioritize model compression, energy-aware execution, and hardware-constrained deployment over default reliance on centralized offloading, especially when targeting devices such as Raspberry Pi Pico W-class hardware or GPU-oriented environments such as CUDA-enabled systems \[19, 20, 42, 43]. In this sense, ecosystems do not merely host innovation; they delimit and enable it by defining the boundary conditions within which optimization and interoperability must occur \[38, 39, 45]. 



MADN can therefore be conceptualized as an architectural bridge across heterogeneous and partially closed digital ecosystems whose governance structures channel innovation through platform specific rules, interfaces, and boundary resources \[39, 45, 46]. Rather than if openness already exists, the framework can leverage APIs and SDKs as practical mechanisms for negotiated interoperability, because these interfaces increasingly structure how external actors access platform capabilities, coordinate services, and extend existing functionality \[43, 44, 46]. Official development environments such as NVIDIA CUDA and Samsung Knox exemplify how contemporary interfaces have evolved beyond simple data exchange toward deeper forms of controlled integration with hardware, security, and management layers \[43, 44]. For MADN, this means interoperability should be pursued not as abstraction detached from existing platforms, but as modular integration that can operate within platform logics while preserving local autonomy and cross-ecosystem coordination \[33, 37, 46]. 



At the same time, platform and ecosystem research consistently shows that digital systems are socio-technical arrangements whose technical architectures are inseparable from governance, incentives, and patterns of human coordination \[39, 45]. Improvements in sensing, data collection, APIs, and computational methods can feed back into subsequent rounds of redesign, making system evolution increasingly iterative and path-dependent rather than purely linear \[19, 21, 46]. Accordingly, MADN should be viewed not only as a technical interoperability mechanism but also as a socio-technical intervention aimed at navigating fragmentation, improving local adaptability, and aligning digital infrastructure with contextual social and economic realities \[34, 35, 45].
